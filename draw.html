How to get the most out of these

Every tutorial here is built the same way, and the method matters more than any individual drawing.

Big shapes first, details last

Each guide starts with one or two large simple shapes — a circle, a bean, a rectangle — that establish how big the whole drawing will be and where it sits on the page. Details come at the very end.

This is the opposite of what most people do naturally, which is to start with an eye, draw outward, and run off the edge of the paper. Blocking in the big shape first makes running out of room essentially impossible.

Sketch light, then commit

The first steps are scaffolding. Draw them lightly, so you can rub them out or draw straight over them without a fight. Once the proportions look right, go over the lines you want to keep with a firmer stroke, and the sketchy construction lines stop being visible.

This is why the early steps are worth doing in pencil even if you plan to ink the final drawing. Confident lines on top of a light sketch look far better than careful lines drawn from nothing.

Draw from the elbow

Long lines drawn with just the fingers come out shaky. Anchor your wrist, move your whole forearm, and go faster than feels safe. Speed produces smooth curves; slowness produces wobble. It feels wrong for about a week and then becomes automatic.

For grown-ups helping a child: resist correcting proportions. A drawing where the head is too big is not a mistake — that is how children draw the thing that matters most to them, and it is the same instinct professional cartoonists use deliberately. Praise the choice rather than fixing it.

What to do when it goes wrong

It will go wrong. Some things worth knowing:

  • If it looks stiff, you have probably drawn everything symmetrical and level. Tilt the body. Bend the neck one way and the tail the other. A gentle S-curve running through a whole creature is what makes it look alive.
  • If it looks flat, let shapes overlap. A near leg drawn in front of a far leg, with the far one partly hidden, creates depth instantly.
  • If it looks fussy, you have added detail everywhere. Pick one area — usually the head or chest — put the detail there, and leave the rest clean.
  • If the face looks wrong, check the eye spacing before anything else. Eyes set too close together is the single most common cause, and moving them apart fixes more drawings than any other adjustment.
  • If you hate it, draw it again immediately rather than repairing it. The second attempt takes a third of the time and is almost always better. Nobody's first version is the good one.

Nothing here needs special equipment

A pencil and paper are enough for every tutorial on this site. If you want to add to that, in order of how much difference it makes:

  1. A softer pencil (a B or 2B) for sketching. It puts down a light line easily and rubs out cleanly.
  2. A kneaded eraser, which lifts graphite instead of smearing it and does not leave crumbs.
  3. A fineliner or felt tip for going over the final lines. The contrast between a sketch and a firm ink line is what makes a drawing look finished.
  4. Colouring pencils last, and only if you want them. A good line drawing does not need colour.

None of it is required. Every shape in these guides is one any pencil can make.

Then bring it into the game

Everything you draw here works as a character in Doodle Heroes, our free browser game. You draw a hero on a paper canvas, and that exact drawing becomes the character you play as in a side-scrolling run. It is free, needs no account, and nothing you draw ever leaves your device.

One thing worth knowing before you do: the game crops tightly to your drawing and keeps its proportions, so the shape of what you drew changes how hard it is to play. Compact drawings are easier; wide, sprawling ones are harder. Our drawing tips page explains exactly how that works.
