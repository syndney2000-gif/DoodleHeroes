Drawing a hero that actually plays well

There is no wrong drawing in this game — a single wobbly circle works fine. But some doodles are noticeably easier to keep alive than others, and the reason is worth knowing.

Last updated 1 August 2026

What the game does with your drawing

The moment you tap My Hero is Ready, the game does something simple and slightly clever: it scans your canvas looking for any pixel you actually painted, ignores every bit of empty paper, and crops tightly to a box around your marks with a few pixels of breathing room.

Three things follow from that, and they explain almost everything about how a doodle behaves in play:

  • Where you draw on the canvas does not matter. Tuck your hero in the bottom corner if you like. The crop finds it. There is no need to centre anything.
  • How big you draw does not matter either. Everything gets scaled to the same height in game. A tiny thumbnail sketch and a drawing that fills the page become the same size hero.
  • The proportions matter enormously. Because the crop preserves your drawing's shape, a wide drawing stays wide and a tall drawing stays tall — and the hero's collision box follows suit.

The one rule that changes your score: width is difficulty. A wide hero has more surface to catch a spike, more overhang to snag a monster, and needs more clean ground to land on after a pit. A compact, roughly square doodle is genuinely easier to play. A long horizontal dragon is genuinely harder.

So: if a younger player is getting frustrated, the fix usually is not more practice. It is drawing a smaller, rounder hero.

Stray marks count as part of your hero

The crop reaches around everything on the canvas. One accidental dot in the far corner stretches the box all the way out to include it — and now your hero is scaled down to fit that invisible extra space, with a chunk of empty air attached.

If your hero suddenly looks tiny or oddly offset in the game, this is why. Tap Clear and start again, or use Undo to walk back the stray stroke. Undo remembers your last twenty strokes, so you have plenty of room to experiment.

Thick lines read better than thin ones

Your drawing gets shrunk down to hero size, and fine detail does not survive that. A carefully hatched cape becomes a grey smudge. Delicate whiskers disappear entirely.

Use the biggest brush you are comfortable with and keep shapes bold and simple. Solid blocks of colour survive the shrink; thin scratchy lines do not. This is the same constraint that makes real sprite art look the way it does, and working inside it is half the fun.

Colour choices

The game world is drawn in warm creams and paper tones. Heroes in strong, saturated colours — deep purple, hot pink, bright teal, black — pop against it and are easy to track when the screen speeds up. Pale yellow on pale cream technically works but you will lose sight of your own hero mid-jump.

Eleven heroes worth trying

For when someone says "I don't know what to draw":

  1. A potato with legs. Compact, round, forgiving. The single best beginner hero in the game.
  2. Your pet, from memory, badly. This is the correct way to do it.
  3. A slice of pizza with a determined face.
  4. The world's angriest cloud.
  5. A rocket cat — ears, whiskers, flames underneath.
  6. Just a face, no body at all. Enormous eyes. It works and it is unsettling.
  7. A robot built entirely from squares.
  8. Someone in your family, drawn as a stick figure, with one exaggerated feature.
  9. A ghost — one wobbly blob and two dots. Takes four seconds.
  10. A dragon with a very long tail, if you want the game to be hard. It will be.
  11. A single dot. Yes, it plays. It is a tiny brave dot and it is nearly impossible to hit.

Games to play with the drawing itself

The drawing screen turns out to be more fun with rules attached. A few that work well with two or more people:

Blind hero

Draw with your eyes closed. Whatever emerges is your hero, no redrawing allowed. Compare scores. The blind heroes usually do fine, which is the joke.

Five seconds

Someone counts to five out loud. Pencils down when they finish. Speed forces the bold, simple shapes that actually play best, so this often produces genuinely good heroes.

Pass the doodle

One person draws the head, hands the device over, the next draws the body, the next adds one detail. Nobody controls the result and everybody has to play it.

Draw the opposite

Try to draw the worst possible hero — as wide and awkward and unaerodynamic as you can manage. Then actually play it. Losing badly on purpose is its own genre of fun.

If it goes wrong

Nothing here is permanent. Undo steps back through your recent strokes, Clear wipes the canvas, and after a run ends you can tap Draw a New Hero to start over from scratch. Heroes are not saved between sessions, so there is no wrong move and nothing to lose — if you love one, screenshot it before you close the tab.
