How to play Doodle Heroes

You draw something. Anything. That drawing becomes the character you control in a side-scrolling run. This page explains every control, every hazard, and exactly how the score adds up.

Last updated 1 August 2026

The one control

There is only one input in the whole game, and it is jump. You can trigger it three ways:

  • Tap anywhere on the screen — the entire play area is the button, so you never have to aim at a small target.
  • Click anywhere with a mouse.
  • Press Space or ↑ on a keyboard.

That is the complete control scheme. There is no run button — your hero runs on their own — no duck, no attack, and no left or right movement. Your hero stays at a fixed spot near the left of the screen while the world scrolls past.

There is no double jump. Your hero only responds to a tap when their feet are on the ground. Tapping frantically in mid-air does nothing at all, and it is the single most common reason a run ends early. Land first, then jump again.

Reading the HUD

Three little cards sit across the top of the play screen:

A hint reading "tap anywhere to jump!" appears when a run begins and fades away after about three and a half seconds.

What is out there

⭐ Stars — collect these

Stars bob gently in the air and are the only thing in the game you actively want to touch. Each one is worth +10 score and +1 star.

They appear two ways. Sometimes you get a single star floating on its own. Other times you get a run of five in a curved arc — those arcs are shaped so that one well-timed jump sweeps up the whole set. If you see an arc coming, jump a little earlier than feels natural so you are rising into the first star rather than falling past it.

You do not have to hit stars dead centre. The pickup radius is generous and scales with how big your hero is, so a near miss usually still counts.

⛰ Pits — jump over these

Pits are gaps in the ground. They are the most dangerous obstacle in the game because they punish hesitation rather than bad timing: if your hero's middle passes over open air while standing, the ground simply stops holding them up and they start to fall.

Falling into a pit costs a heart. Once you are below the bottom of the screen it is already too late — there is no recovery jump. Commit early.

▲ Spikes — jump over these

Spikes are low, narrow, and sit flat on the ground. Touching one anywhere costs a heart. They are the friendliest hazard in the game: they are short enough that almost any jump clears them, so the only real way to hit one is to be in the middle of landing from a previous jump.

👾 Monsters — jump over or land on

Monsters are the interesting one, because they are the only obstacle you can turn into points.

Run into a monster from the side and you lose a heart, same as anything else. But come down on top of one while you are falling and you squash it instead: you get +50 score, the monster tumbles off the bottom of the screen, and your hero bounces back up into a fresh jump.

The catch is in the word falling. The stomp only registers if your hero is genuinely on the way down and their feet are above the monster's head at the moment of contact. Clipping a monster on the way up, or drifting into it sideways at the top of a jump, counts as a hit. Jump early so you are descending onto it, not rising into it.

That bounce is worth learning properly. A successful stomp launches you higher than a normal jump, which means a monster sitting just before a pit is not a double threat — it is a free boost across the gap.

How scoring actually works

The survival tick is worth understanding, because it is not a flat rate — it is tied to your current speed. The faster the game gets, the more points every second of survival is worth. Late in a run you are earning noticeably quicker than you were at the start, which means distance beats greed. A star is 10 points; risking a heart for one when you are deep into a fast run is usually a bad trade.

The results screen shows three numbers: final score, stars collected, and distance in metres.

The difficulty curve

Two things ramp up as you run, and they ramp independently.

Speed. The world scrolls at a steady starting pace, then nudges faster roughly every five seconds. It keeps climbing until it hits a ceiling of a bit over twice the starting speed, and then holds there. So there is a top speed — surviving the first minute or so is the hard part, and after that the pace stops getting worse. Many players never find this out because they assume it accelerates forever.

Spawn rate. Obstacles also arrive closer together the further you travel. The gap between spawns is partly random, so the rhythm never becomes fully predictable, but the average gap tightens with distance.

Together these mean the run does not have a wall so much as a slow squeeze. Your reaction window shrinks from comfortable to tight, and eventually you meet a pit you commit to a beat too late.

After you get hit

Losing a heart is not instantly fatal. You get about a second and a half of invulnerability, during which your hero flickers and cannot be hurt again. Use it. If you took a hit inside a cluster of obstacles, keep running through the rest of the cluster rather than panicking — those next couple of hazards are free.

Six things that will get you further

  1. Jump early, not late. Almost every death in this game is a jump that came a fraction too late. Your hero has real weight and takes time to get off the ground.
  2. Stop tapping in mid-air. It does nothing, and it trains the wrong instinct.
  3. Watch the right edge, not your hero. Your hero never moves horizontally, so there is nothing to learn by looking at them. All the information arrives from the right.
  4. Treat monsters as trampolines. A stomp is 50 points and free height. Once the timing clicks, monsters become the best thing on the field.
  5. Skip stars that sit low over a pit. Ten points is never worth a heart.
  6. Draw a smaller hero. Genuinely — see below.

Your drawing changes the difficulty

This is the part players miss, and it matters more than any technique on this page.

When you finish drawing, the game finds the edges of your doodle and crops tightly to them, then scales the result to a fixed height. The shape of what you drew survives that process — so a tall thin drawing becomes a tall thin hero, and a wide sprawling drawing becomes a wide hero.

Width is what costs you. Your hero's collision box follows the drawing's proportions, so a wide hero has more area to catch a spike, more overhang to snag a monster, and needs a longer clean landing strip after a pit. A compact, roughly square doodle is meaningfully easier to keep alive than a long horizontal one.

If you want the easiest possible character, draw something small and tight. If you want a challenge, draw a dragon with an enormous tail. Both are completely valid — but now you know which one you picked.
