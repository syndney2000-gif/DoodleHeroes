How to draw a cat

Almost every cartoon cat in the world is two circles wearing triangles. Once you see the structure underneath, you can draw one from any angle, in any mood, in about a minute.

Difficulty: easy Time: 5–10 minutes Ages: 4 and up with help, 7 and up alone

Last updated 2 August 2026

What you need

A pencil, something to rub out with, and paper. That is genuinely it. A pencil matters more than you would think for the first three steps, because those steps are scaffolding you are going to erase — trying to build them in pen is how people end up with a cat covered in construction lines they cannot remove.

If you are drawing on a screen instead, the same order works. Sketch the circles on a low opacity, draw the real lines on top, then delete the sketch layer.

The six steps

Why the ears go on top

This is the single thing that separates a cat from a bear, and most people get it wrong on the first try. Human ears are on the sides of the head, so that is where our hands want to put them. Cat ears sit high on the skull, close together, pointing up and very slightly outward.

A useful check: imagine the top of your circle is a clock face. The ears should start at about ten o'clock and two o'clock, not nine and three. Move them down the sides and you have drawn a bear. Move them further apart and flop them over and you have drawn a dog.

Make them bigger than you think. Cartoon cat ears are enormous — often a third of the head's height. Timid little ears read as a mouse. When in doubt, draw them taller.

Eyes: the lemon shape

Round eyes make a cat look permanently startled, which is a legitimate choice but probably not what you want by default. The classic cat eye is a lemon or leaf shape: two curves meeting at points on the left and right.

You can drive the entire mood of the drawing from these two shapes without touching anything else:

  • Wide lemons with big round pupils — friendly, kittenish, pleased to see you.
  • Narrow slits, just two curved lines — smug, sleepy, or plotting.
  • Lemons with the top edge flattened into a straight line — unimpressed. This is the classic cat-judging-you face.
  • One eye a full circle, one a slit — a wink, and instantly a cartoon.

Leave a small white dot in each pupil, always in the same corner of both eyes. That single dot is what makes the eyes look wet and alive rather than like two holes.

The tail carries the personality

Real cats signal their whole emotional state with their tail, and drawings inherit that. A tail is one line thickened into a shape, so it costs you almost nothing and changes everything:

  • Curled up in a question mark — happy and confident. The friendliest option.
  • Wrapped around the front paws — content, settled, not going anywhere.
  • Straight up like a flagpole — alert, greeting you.
  • Fat and bristled, drawn with a spiky zigzag edge — alarmed. Pair it with wide round eyes.
  • Low and straight behind — stalking something.

Keep it thick where it joins the body and taper it toward the tip. A tail that is the same width all the way along looks like a rope someone attached.

Four mistakes that stop it looking like a cat

  1. A visible neck. Sitting cats have none. Overlap the head and body circles and never draw a line between them.
  2. Whiskers from the cheeks. They grow from the muzzle, in a tight cluster either side of the nose. Spread them across the whole face and it reads as a walrus.
  3. A dog's nose. Cat noses are tiny — a small downward triangle. A big rounded oval nose is a puppy, every time.
  4. Four separate legs on a sitting cat. You cannot see them. Bumps for the front paws along the base of the body is all you need, and it looks far more convincing.

Once the basic cat works

The two-circle skeleton is a starting point, not a rule. Stretch the body oval sideways and you get a loaf-shaped cat asleep on a windowsill. Shrink the body and enlarge the head and you get a kitten — young animals have proportionally huge heads, which is exactly why they look cute.

Add fur by breaking your outline: instead of one smooth curve around the body, draw it as a series of small overlapping arcs, like the edge of a cloud. Do it on the chest and cheeks and leave the rest smooth, or the whole drawing gets noisy.

For markings, remember that tabby stripes wrap around the body rather than running along it, so they should curve with the form. Draw a few on the tail as rings. Patches on a calico look best when they are asymmetric — one over an eye, one on the opposite flank.

Try it in the game

A cat is one of the better heroes in Doodle Heroes because the compact two-circle body scales down into a nearly square sprite, which is the shape that plays best. The tail is the one thing to watch: a long horizontal tail widens the drawing, and width is what catches on spikes. Curl it up over the back instead of sweeping it out sideways and you keep the personality without paying for it. There is more on why in our guide to drawing heroes that play well.
