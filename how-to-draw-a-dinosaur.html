How to draw a dinosaur

One egg shape gets you every dinosaur there is. What changes between a T. rex and a Stegosaurus is almost entirely the neck and the bits along the back.

Difficulty: easy to medium Time: 10 minutes Ages: 5 and up

Last updated 2 August 2026

The one rule that makes a dinosaur stand up

Before any steps: a two-legged dinosaur balances like a see-saw. The hips are the pivot. Whatever weight is in front of the hips — chest, neck, head — has to be matched by tail behind them.

This is why cartoon T. rexes drawn standing upright like a person, tail dragging on the floor, always look wrong. They look like they are about to fall forward, because they would. Tip the whole body horizontal, put the head out in front and the tail out behind, and the drawing becomes convincing immediately.

The tail almost never touches the ground. It is a counterweight held out in the air.

The six steps: a T. rex

The backwards knee

The joint you can see halfway up a dinosaur's leg, bending the wrong way, is not a knee. It is an ankle. The real knee is tucked up inside the body outline, hidden.

You do not need to understand the anatomy to use this — just draw the leg as a zigzag: down and forward from the hip, back and down to that high ankle, then forward again to a flat foot. Three segments. Birds have exactly the same arrangement, which is not a coincidence, and looking at a chicken's leg is genuinely the best reference available.

Make the thigh thick. It held up several tonnes. A spindly thigh is the fastest way to make a T. rex look like a lizard.

Feet point forward, toes spread. Three toes visible from the side, with a small claw on each. A flat blob foot works fine at small sizes, but three toes take two extra seconds and look much better.

Same egg, different dinosaur

Everything above is one body shape. Change the neck and the back and you get the rest of them.

Stegosaurus

Take the egg, make it rounder and put it on four legs — short and stumpy at the front, taller at the back, so the hips are the highest point. Give it a tiny head on a short low neck, held near the ground.

The plates are the whole character. Draw them as tall rounded triangles, biggest over the hips and shrinking toward both the head and the tail tip. Do not put them in a single neat row — stagger them in two offset rows, which is both more accurate and much better looking. Finish with four spikes on the tail, arranged in two pairs.

Triceratops

Four sturdy legs and a bulky body, then almost all the effort goes into the head. Draw the skull as a wide shape, then a large curved frill fanning up and back behind it like a shield. Scallop the frill's edge and it reads as bone.

Three horns: one short one on the nose, two long ones above the eyes sweeping forward and slightly up. The beak at the front of the mouth curves down like a parrot's. Keep the tail short — this one is not a counterweight animal.

Brachiosaurus and the long-necks

Start with the same egg, but stand it up so the shoulders are much higher than the hips. Four thick pillar legs, like an elephant's, with the front pair longer than the back.

The neck is one long sweeping curve going up and slightly forward, thick where it joins the body and tapering all the way. The head at the end is tiny — smaller than you will want to draw it. That is the whole joke of the animal, so lean into it. Balance the neck with a long tail sweeping back the other way.

Pterosaurs and the sea ones

Strictly, neither is a dinosaur — pterosaurs flew and plesiosaurs swam, and both are cousins rather than members. Worth knowing, and children who like dinosaurs tend to love this fact.

Draw a pterosaur as a wide V of wings with a long crested head, and the wing membrane stretched from an enormously long fourth finger. A plesiosaur is a turtle body with four paddles and a very long neck.

Texture without spending an hour

Do not draw scales all over. A few short curved lines where the leg meets the body, and a couple across the belly, suggest folded skin and imply texture across the whole animal.

If you want feathers — and the evidence says many of them had feathers — draw them as a fringe along the back of the neck, the arms and the tail tip, breaking the outline into soft points. A feathered T. rex is more accurate than a bald one and looks better.
