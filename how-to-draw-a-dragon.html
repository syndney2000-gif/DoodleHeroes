How to draw a dragon

Dragons look like the hardest thing on the page and are secretly one of the easiest. Nobody has ever seen one, so nobody can tell you yours is wrong.

Difficulty: medium Time: 10–15 minutes Ages: 6 and up

Last updated 2 August 2026

The idea behind this one

The trap with dragons is starting at the head. You draw a beautiful snout, then run out of paper before the tail, and the body ends up cramped into whatever space is left.

So we build the body first — one fat bean shape in the middle of the page — and hang everything else off it. The bean sets the scale for the whole animal, and once it is down you cannot run out of room.

The six steps

Wings are the part everyone dreads

They should not be. A dragon wing is a bat wing, and a bat wing is an umbrella that has lost a fight.

Draw the top edge first, as a single arc going up and out from the shoulder. Then, instead of closing it with a smooth line, come back underneath in a series of scoops — down, up, down, up — like the bottom of a curtain. Each scoop ends where a rib comes down. Finally draw two or three straight ribs from the shoulder out to the point of each scoop.

That is the whole trick. The scalloped edge is what makes it read as leathery instead of feathered. Three scoops is plenty; more than four starts to look fussy at small sizes.

One wing or two? If your dragon is side-on, draw one wing properly and let the far wing be a smaller shape peeking over the back. Two identical wings side by side flattens the drawing and makes it look like a logo.

Pick a head shape and commit

The head is where a dragon gets its species. Three that work well, all built on the same rounded skull:

  • The box snout. A rectangle stuck on the front, like a crocodile. Reads as strong and a bit dim. Good for friendly dragons.
  • The wedge. A triangle tapering to a point at the nose, closer to a lizard. Reads as fast and sharp.
  • The horse head. A long curved muzzle with a gentle dip in the middle. This is the eastern-dragon shape and looks noble rather than fierce.

Whichever you pick, put two dots or slits on the end of the snout for nostrils. It costs one second and it is the difference between a snout and a shoe.

Horns should sweep back from the skull, following the line of the neck. Horns pointing forward look like a bull, and horns pointing straight up look like a party hat.

Spikes, scales and the tail

A row of triangles down the spine, from the back of the head all the way to the tail tip, is the fastest way to make a lizard shape look draconic. Vary the sizes — big in the middle over the shoulders, smaller toward the tail — because a perfectly even row looks mechanical.

Do not draw every scale. A dragon covered in individually drawn scales takes an hour and looks worse than one with a few suggested in one or two places. Put a small patch of overlapping U-shapes on the chest and shoulder and leave the rest of the body clean. Your eye fills in the rest.

The belly is usually a different texture: draw a series of horizontal bands down the underside of the neck and stomach, like a snake. This one detail does more work than scales ever will.

If you want it breathing fire

Fire is drawn as one shape, not as flames. Start with a long triangle coming out of the mouth and widening as it goes. Then make its edges wobble in and out. Inside that shape, draw a second, smaller wobbly shape following the same direction.

If you are colouring, the inner shape is the hot one — yellow or white — and the outer shape is orange or red. Fire is brightest in the middle, and getting that one relationship right matters more than the shape of any individual flame.

Common problems

  1. The neck is too thin. It has to hold up a head full of teeth. Make it at least as thick as the head is tall where it meets the body.
  2. The legs are too long. Cartoon dragons look best on short, stumpy legs. Long legs read as a horse in a costume.
  3. The wings are too small. A wing should be roughly as long as the body. Small wings look decorative rather than functional.
  4. Everything is symmetrical. Bend the neck one way and sweep the tail the other. An S-curve through the whole animal is what makes it look alive.

Try it in the game

Fair warning: a dragon is the hardest hero to play in Doodle Heroes, and that is a deliberate feature of how the game works. It crops tightly to your drawing and keeps the proportions, so a long tail and outstretched wings make a very wide sprite — and a wide sprite catches spikes and needs more clear ground to land on.

If you want the dragon and a decent score, draw it compact: tail curled up alongside the body, wings folded. If you want the challenge, sprawl it out. Either is a legitimate choice, and the drawing tips page explains exactly why the shape matters.
