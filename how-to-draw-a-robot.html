How to draw a robot

Robots are the best drawing exercise for anyone who thinks they cannot draw, because a robot is allowed to be made entirely of shapes you already know how to make.

Difficulty: easy Time: 10 minutes Ages: 4 and up

Last updated 2 August 2026

Why robots are the friendliest thing to draw

With an animal, there is a correct answer and your drawing can be measurably wrong. A robot has no correct answer. Nobody can tell you a robot's arms are the wrong length, because somebody built it that way on purpose.

That makes it the ideal place to practise something that matters in every drawing: varying your shapes. The reason a first robot often looks like a fridge is not bad drawing, it is that every box is roughly the same size.

The six steps

The fridge problem

Here is the thing that fixes most beginner robots in one move: make every box a noticeably different size.

If the head, body, upper arm and lower arm are all roughly similar rectangles, the eye reads the whole thing as one lumpy object. Exaggerate instead. A tiny head on an enormous barrel body. A huge head on a spindly little frame. Massive forearms and skinny upper arms.

The contrast is what makes it read as a designed machine rather than a stack of boxes. Pick one part to be dramatically bigger than everything else and let it dominate.

Round the corners. A rectangle with slightly rounded corners immediately looks friendlier and more manufactured than a sharp-cornered one. Sharp corners suit a menacing robot; rounded ones suit a helpful one. Choose deliberately.

Two ways to build the limbs

Segmented arms are boxes and cylinders with visible gaps at the joints, often with a small circle drawn at each elbow and shoulder to show the pivot. This looks solid and mechanical. Leave a real gap between segments — that gap is what says "these parts move separately."

Bendy arms are drawn as a spring or a stack of stretched ovals, like a vacuum cleaner hose. They can curve into any pose, which makes them much easier to make expressive. Draw a single curved line for the path of the arm, then draw a series of shallow arcs crossing it.

Bendy arms are more forgiving for beginners because they do not need to be the same length on both sides, and any curve you make looks intentional.

For hands: two-fingered claws are the fastest and read instantly as robotic. Circular suction pads, a magnet, or a small drill all work. A five-fingered human hand almost never looks right on a boxy robot.

Giving a face made of shapes a real expression

A robot face is a handful of shapes on a flat panel, which sounds limiting and is not. Everything comes from the eyebrow line — even though robots do not have eyebrows.

  • Two flat lines angled down toward the centre — angry or determined.
  • Angled up toward the centre — worried or sad.
  • No lines at all, just round eyes — blank, innocent, slightly unsettling.
  • One eye bigger than the other — curious, or broken. Both are good.

The mouth does less work than you would expect. A grille of vertical lines is neutral and always safe. A single horizontal line that curves slightly at one end suggests a mood without committing to a full expression. A jagged zigzag reads as a grin or a grimace depending entirely on those eyebrow lines above it.

If you want one eye instead of two, make it large and central, and put a smaller circle inside it for a lens. Single-eyed robots read as more machine and less person.

Details that sell it

A few small things, applied sparingly, do most of the work:

  • Bolts. Small circles at the corners of panels. Four per panel, no more.
  • A seam line running around the body, suggesting it opens.
  • A dial or gauge — a circle with a single needle line inside it.
  • An antenna with a ball on the end. Bend it slightly; a bent antenna has history.
  • Vents — three or four short parallel lines on a shoulder or side panel.

Concentrate all of it on the chest and head, and leave the arms and legs mostly plain. Detail everywhere is the same as detail nowhere.

Try it in the game

A robot is a strong hero for Doodle Heroes if you keep the arms in. The game crops to the outer edges of your drawing, so arms flung out sideways make a wide sprite that catches spikes, while arms held down at the sides keep the outline compact and roughly square — the easiest shape to play.

Big feet are free, since they sit under the body rather than beside it. A tall antenna is also free, because height costs you nothing. The drawing tips page explains the full logic.
