How to draw a rocket

A rocket is three shapes stacked in a line. It is the most forgiving thing in this whole series, and the easiest place to learn that straight lines want a ruler-free wobble.

Difficulty: very easy Time: 5 minutes Ages: 3 and up with help, 5 and up alone

Last updated 2 August 2026

The three-part rule

Every convincing cartoon rocket has the same three parts, in the same order, top to bottom: a nose cone, a tube, and fins. Get those three in place and the drawing works even if nothing else is added.

The proportion that matters most is the tube. Make it about twice as tall as the nose cone. Shorter and the rocket looks like a traffic cone; much longer and it looks like a pencil.

The five steps

Why your straight lines should wobble

The instinct with a rocket is to reach for a ruler. Resist it. A hand-drawn line that bows very slightly has life in it; a ruled line looks like a technical diagram and, oddly, less convincing.

The exception is if you are going for a deliberately clean, flat, poster-style look — then rule everything and be consistent about it. What looks wrong is a mixture: two ruled edges and two wobbly ones. Pick one and stick to it for the whole drawing.

Draw the long lines with your whole arm, not your fingers. Anchor your wrist, move from the elbow, and go faster than feels comfortable. Slow lines wobble in an ugly way; fast lines curve smoothly. This is the single most transferable drawing habit on this page.

Fins: the mistake almost everyone makes

Fins get drawn on the sides of the tube, level with its bottom edge. That is not quite right, and the drawing looks flat as a result.

Fins should start partway up the tube and extend below its base. That overhang is what makes the rocket look like it is standing on them, and it gives you the little gap underneath where the exhaust comes out.

For a rocket seen straight on, draw a third fin as a narrow triangle in the middle of the tube, pointing down. Your brain reads it as the far side of the rocket and the whole thing becomes three-dimensional for the cost of one triangle.

Windows and panels

One porthole, high on the tube, is the classic. Draw a circle, then a second circle around it — that outer ring reads as a metal rim and stops the window looking like a hole. If you want a face at the window, keep it tiny; a single curve and two dots is enough.

A single horizontal band around the tube suggests it is built from sections. Two bands is fine. Five bands and it stops looking like a rocket and starts looking like a stack of tins.

Resist the urge to cover the body in rivets and hatches. Detail is best used in one concentrated area with plenty of clean space around it. A rocket with one carefully drawn porthole and an otherwise empty hull looks better than one detailed all over.

Exhaust that reads as speed

Flames are drawn as a single shape with a wobbly edge, not as separate individual flames. Start at one bottom corner of the rocket, dip down into a point, come back up, dip down further into a longer point in the middle, and back up to the other corner.

Make the middle point the longest. Symmetrical flames look static; an uneven outline suggests movement.

For extra speed, add a few short straight lines in the empty space to either side of the rocket, running parallel to its body. These are speed lines and they cost nothing. Two or three per side is plenty — any more and the page gets busy.

Smoke, if you want it, is a cluster of overlapping bumpy circles below the flames, getting bigger as they get further away.

Design your own spacecraft

Once the basic rocket works, the shape is a toy. Some directions worth trying:

  • The flying saucer. Two shallow curves for the dish and a dome on top. Add three landing legs with round feet.
  • The bottle rocket. Very wide at the bottom, tapering steeply. Looks fast even standing still.
  • The lunar lander. A boxy, awkward body on four spindly legs. Nothing about it should look aerodynamic — that is the point, since it never flies in air.
  • The shuttle. A rocket lying on its side with wings instead of fins.
  • Something that should not fly. A teapot with fins. A shoe with a porthole. Add the three parts to any object and it becomes a spacecraft.

Try it in the game

A rocket is an excellent hero for Doodle Heroes. Because the game crops tightly to your drawing and scales it to a fixed height, a tall narrow rocket becomes a tall narrow sprite — and narrow is exactly what you want. There is very little width to catch on spikes.

Draw it pointing up rather than lying on its side, keep the fins tucked in close, and you will have one of the easier heroes to keep alive. The drawing tips page goes into why width costs you and height does not.
